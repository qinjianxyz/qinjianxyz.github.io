04 / 05 · Ambition before recognition

Ninety Thousand Li Into the Sky

扶摇九万里

The great Peng rises with the wind. Even when the wind dies down, it can still stir the sea.

LITERARY SOURCE

To Li Yong

Li Bai · Tang dynasty

大鹏一日同风起，

扶摇直上九万里。

假令风歇时下来，

犹能簸却沧溟水。

世人见我恒殊调，

闻余大言皆冷笑。

宣父犹能畏后生，

丈夫未可轻年少。

English translation

Translated with AI assistance. Read alongside the original Chinese poem.

One day the great Peng rises with the wind,

spiraling straight up, ninety thousand li.

Even if the wind should cease and it descend,

it could still shake the waters of the vast sea.

People find my voice forever out of step;

they hear my bold words and answer with cold laughter.

Even Confucius held the young in awe—

one should never look lightly on youth.

READING THE POEM

Context & imagery

Traditionally attributed to Li Bai, To Li Yong draws on the enormous Peng bird in the Zhuangzi’s “Free and Easy Wandering,” turning an ancient image into youthful confidence. Its closing lines recall Confucius’s regard for younger generations: those not yet recognized should not be dismissed. The poem is often linked to Li Bai’s youth, but its precise occasion is not established here. A li is a traditional unit of distance; ninety thousand evokes a mythic scale.

FROM POEM TO IMAGE

The poem in the image

Wings, wind, and sea give ambition a visible form. As the bird rises, the ocean seems to move with it. Its vast scale echoes the boldness of youthful ambition before it has won recognition.
