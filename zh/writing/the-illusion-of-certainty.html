确定性的幻觉

The Illusion of Certainty

从医疗、工作与 AI 谈起，思考二元思维、身份认同，以及在不确定中保留判断与行动的能力。

中文译文 · AI 辅助翻译

我最近经常在想，我们是多么容易把世界划分为相互对立的两边。

资本主义还是社会主义。中国还是美国。男人还是女人。工作还是懒惰。全民医疗还是财政责任。Bitcoin 还是法定货币。AI 会拯救我们，还是毁灭我们。

我们听到一个论点，几乎会自动寻找它的对立面。如果有人批评资本主义，人们就认为他支持社会主义。如果有人说中国在某件事上做得不错，人们就认为他在为中国政府辩护。如果有人质疑工作的意义，人们就认为他在为懒惰辩护。如果有人支持让更多人获得医疗服务，就会被指责忽视成本；如果谈论成本，又会被指责不关心人。

起初，我以为这只是二元思维的问题。但我越来越觉得，更深层的问题是确定性。

二元思维有吸引力，是因为确定性有吸引力。世界很复杂，而复杂的系统让人不安。它们迫使我们承认：两件相互矛盾的事可能同时为真；我们不喜欢的人，仍然可能在某件事上是对的；政策可能解决一个问题，同时制造另一个问题；有时，我们不得不在不知道结果的情况下做决定。

二元框架消除了其中很大一部分不适。一旦决定自己站在哪一边，很多思考就已经被替我们完成了。

问题是，现实不会因为我们这样描述它，就变得更简单。

医疗

实现财务独立之后，我花了更多时间研究自己关心的议题，只因为我认为它们重要。医疗就是其中之一。我也对人类学，以及社会如何决定人与人之间应当承担什么义务这个更广泛的问题，产生了更多兴趣。

医疗尤其值得研究，因为它的道德问题起初看起来很简单。如果一个社会拥有救人的医生、药物、设备和知识，却因为一个人付不起治疗费用而让他死去，这似乎很荒谬。我们谈论的是一条人命。如果治疗方法存在，而且能够提供，钱却成了唯一的阻碍，这本身就深具悲剧性。

这就是全民医疗的主张如此有说服力的原因。

但另一个事实依然成立：医疗非常昂贵。

如果我们不改变制度中的任何东西，只承诺支付每一张账单，就没有真正解决问题，只是改变了由谁来付钱。医院定价、药品定价、行政开支、激励机制、保险结构、医疗服务的使用，以及临终照护，依然都很重要。一个社会可以非常在意让人活下来，同时仍然面对一个现实限制：究竟能把多少资源投入医疗。

我觉得奇怪的是，这为什么会变成如此两极化的争论。支持全民医疗，不需要假装成本不重要。关心成本，也不需要对一个付不起治疗费的病人漠不关心。

这两种现实同时存在。

更好的问题，不只是我们是否支持全民医疗，而是如何确保人们能够获得必要的医疗服务，同时建立一个在财务上能够持续运转的系统。这比选边站更难，但至少这才是真正的问题。

资本主义与社会主义

资本主义与社会主义之间的争论，常常给我同样的感觉。

现代国家都不是某一种制度的纯粹范例。美国有私人市场，但也有社会保障、联邦医疗保险（Medicare）、医疗补助（Medicaid）、公共教育、存款保险、农业补贴、政府科研、基础设施支出，以及庞大的公共部门。中国有国有制和产业政策，但也有私营企业、亿万富翁、资本市场、创业者和激烈的竞争。

大多数国家都是混合体，因为社会本身就是混合体。

市场能很好地解决一些问题。政府能解决一些市场解决不好的问题。政府也会失灵。市场也会失灵。家庭、社区、慈善组织、教会和地方机构所做的一些事情，是政府和市场都难以轻易替代的。

所以，我越来越不在意一个国家把自己称为资本主义、社会主义，还是别的什么。标签不是结果。

重要的是，人们的生活实际上发生了什么。

普通人能否建立体面的生活？能否拥有财产？能否创业？能否养育孩子？能否表达？能否迁徙？能否在不必时时恐惧的情况下参与社会？有权势的机构能否欺压他们而不承担后果？当制度犯下严重错误时，这个错误能否得到纠正？

政府最严重的失败通常是具体的，而不是理论上的。无辜者遭到迫害。财富被不公正地夺走。战争被发动。权力被滥用。人们被迫噤声。机构保护自己，而不是保护本应服务的人。

每个大国都有这样的例子，东方和西方都有。没有一种文明会仅仅因为用正确的意识形态描述自己，就可以免于这些问题。

我有中国人的成长背景，成年后的很大一部分时间生活在美国。对我来说，一个中国人说美国在某件事上做得更好，或者一个美国人说中国在某件事上做得更好，都应该完全正常。这不该威胁到任何一方的身份认同。

事实上，能否承认那个你被期待去反对的阵营的长处，或许正是检验自己有没有独立思考的一种有效方式。

如果一个事实因为说出它的人是谁而显得危险，我们或许已经不再评估事实本身了。

工作

关于工作的争论是另一个例子。

年长一代常说年轻人懒，或者不再愿意工作。年轻人则常常看着自己能找到的工作，觉得其中许多并不值得自己投入那么多时间、承受那么大压力、作出那么多牺牲。

争论通常很快就变成道德问题。

一方说人们失去了自律。另一方说这个制度具有剥削性。

但在判断谁对之前，我认为应该先问一个更基本的问题：工作究竟是为了什么？

美国劳工统计局预测，2024 年至 2034 年间，美国就业岗位总数将增长 3.1%，从约 1.7 亿个增至 1.752 亿个。预计新增岗位主要集中在医疗与社会援助，以及专业、科学和技术服务领域。若干行业预计会减少岗位，其中相当一部分降幅集中在零售业。

这告诉了我们一些关于劳动力市场的信息，却远不足以说明人们实际上将如何生活。

即使十年后美国有 1.75 亿个工作岗位，那会是什么样的工作？薪酬多少？需要工作多少小时？有多稳定？位于哪里？人们能否靠这些工作负担住房、医疗、育儿和退休生活？劳动者有多大的议价能力？真正想做这些新增工作的人有多少？

就业岗位数量并不等于人的福祉。

更重要的是，一份受雇的工作不等于劳动，而劳动也不等于价值。

养育孩子是劳动。照顾年迈的父母是劳动。种植供人食用的作物是劳动。维护一个家是劳动。帮助社区里的人是劳动。钻研困难的东西是劳动。开发软件、写作、创作艺术或教别人知识，即使没有雇主参与，也可以创造价值。

就业是组织劳动和分配收入的一种方式。它不是有意义的人类活动的唯一形式。

不知从什么时候开始，现代社会把几件事紧紧绑在了一起：工作、收入、身份、尊严和有用性。因此，失业给人的感觉，可能不只是失去收入。人们感到难堪，觉得自己的价值降低了，也可能不知道该怎样回答“你是做什么的？”这个问题。

把如此多的意义寄托在一份雇佣合同上，是一件奇怪的事。

我并不是在主张人应该什么都不做。我们享用的几乎一切，都来自某个人的努力。食物需要生产，房屋需要建造，道路需要维护，病人需要照顾，孩子需要养育，社区需要用心经营。

但劳动是我们创造和维持美好生活的一种手段。它不应该成为生活本身的目的。

随着 AI 发展，这一点变得更重要。未来的技术很可能让社会用更少的人类劳动时间，生产更多商品和服务。如果真是如此，仅仅因为我们习惯围绕就业来组织社会，就保留不必要的劳动，这会是一种奇怪的进步定义。

目标不应该是确保每个人始终忙碌。

目标应该是让人们更容易过好生活、作出贡献、创造、关心他人，并拥有有意义的人生。

AI 与价值创造的确定性

这引出了 AI 的一个方面，我觉得尤其有意思。

AI 不只是在改变人们能创造什么。它也可能在改变人们有多大把握能够有意地创造价值。

以迪士尼为例。

像迪士尼这样的公司，可以为一部电影投入数亿美元，因为它经过几十年建立起了一整套体系。它可以雇用经验丰富的编剧、动画师、演员、制片人和营销人员。它有发行渠道，有品牌知名度。它可以测试想法、研究观众，并把成品呈现给数百万人。

这一切都不能保证成功。迪士尼仍然可能拍出烂片，或者误判人们想看什么。但这套体系降低了不确定性。大公司可以花足够多的钱，雇用足够多有经验的人，利用足够强的发行能力，来提高最终产出有价值之物的概率。

资本购买的不只是质量，也包括一定程度的确定性。

现在，想象一个拥有 AI 工具的普通十七岁少年。

他没有工作室，没有电影学院背景，没有营销部门，也没有真正的商业计划。也许他做了个东西，只是因为觉得好玩。他把它发到网上，不知怎么就有数百万人观看。

他可能在一个晚上创造出的娱乐价值，比一个专业团队有意追求同样目标时创造的还多。

但故事还有另一面：他可能不知道为什么成功了，也可能无法再次做到。

这一点才是我认为重要的。

AI 让人们更容易创造出过去需要资金、团队和专门技能才能做出的东西。这扩大了能够产出有价值之物的人群。但当数百万人突然获得这种能力时，事先判断哪个作品会产生影响，也可能变得更难。

我们意外创造价值的能力，增长速度可能正在超过我们有意创造价值的能力。

这并不等于说价值正在消失。在很多领域，发生的可能恰恰相反：有用的软件、娱乐、知识、艺术和实验，可能比以往多得多。

可能正在下降的，是我们对于努力、资本和经验能够如预期般创造价值的把握。

一家公司可以花更多钱，却仍然无人问津。一个默默无闻的创作者可以几乎不花钱，却触达数百万人。一位经验丰富的专业人士或许仍然拥有巨大优势，但这种优势已不能保证他与其他人拉开像以前那样大的距离。

稀缺的东西，可能只是转移了。

当内容生产变得更容易，注意力就变得更重要。

当信息变得充裕，信任就更加重要。

当技术执行变得更便宜，判断力和品味就更有价值。

当人人都能创造，知道什么值得创造就变得更难。

这改变的不只是娱乐业。很长一段时间里，我们依赖着一些感觉相对稳定的关系：更多教育往往带来更好的工作，更多经验往往让一个人更有价值，更多员工让公司产出更多，更多资本则让某些项目成为可能。

这些关系不会一夜之间消失，但它们可能会减弱。

一个小团队如今可以完成过去需要大得多的公司才能完成的工作。一个年轻人可以获得过去只有专业人士才拥有的工具。一个不属于大型机构的研究者，可以运用过去需要实验室或大型组织才能具备的能力。

这创造了更多机会，但也可能带来更多不确定性。

人们可能变得更有能力，却更不确定这些能力如何转化为收入。公司可能提高了生产力，却更不确定自己的产品能否吸引并留住注意力。整个社会可能创造出更多价值，却更难知道谁将获得这些价值。

这并非显然的好事或坏事。它是经济结构的一种变化。

赢，究竟意味着什么？

当人们谈论国家之间的 AI 竞争时，同样的问题也会出现。

我们经常听到，美国必须在 AI 上击败中国，或者中国必须赶上美国。这种措辞听起来很像核军备竞赛。

但赢究竟意味着什么？

如果美国建成了世界上最强大的 AI 模型，结果却是一场让世界更不稳定的永久军备竞赛，这算赢吗？

如果中国发展出非凡的 AI 能力，但依赖的是一个需要进一步集中权力、加强监控的制度，这算赢吗？

如果 AI 让 GDP 大幅增长，却削弱了普通劳动者的议价能力，我们应该自动把它叫作成功吗？

如果 AI 让我们以少得多的人类劳动生产更多东西，但我们的社会制度仍要求人们必须有工作才能获得收入、医疗和尊严，那么技术可能成功了，它周围的制度却失败了。

在问谁赢之前，值得先问一问：我们究竟在玩什么游戏？

这个问题的用处远不止于 AI。

一个国家可以变得更富有，人民的生活却更不安稳。

一家公司可以变得更高效，产品却变得更差。

一个医疗系统可以花更多钱，却带来糟糕的结果。

一个人可以挣得更多，却拥有更少的时间、自由和快乐。

指标并不总是目标。

AI 可以同时集中化与去中心化

AI 本身，也是二元框架失效的另一个地方。

一些人说 AI 正在让能力普及，因为一个人如今就能获得过去只有大型组织才具备的能力。这是真的。个人可以越来越广泛地借助 AI，让它充当程序员、研究者、翻译、设计师、分析师和导师。它能赋予个人多大的能力，再怎么强调也不为过。

与此同时，构建最先进的 AI 系统，需要海量资本、芯片、电力、基础设施和数据中心容量。这些资源高度集中。

所以，AI 可以让能力分散，同时让基础设施集中。

这并不矛盾。

同一种技术，可以赋予个人更多力量，同时也增强那些控制最大模型和计算资源的组织的力量。

区块链的架构不同，但更广泛的启示相似。技术不会自动带来某种政治结果。它改变的是可能性。制度、激励与所有权，决定这些可能性如何被使用。

我们为什么喜欢确定性

我理解人们为什么不喜欢不确定性。

我一直是那种面对问题时，会先找出自己能够改变什么的人。如果一件事做得不好，我的本能就是反思、纠正和改进。只要相信自己能采取行动，我从来不觉得自己的缺点特别可怕。

这种心态有用，因为它让失败变成暂时的事情。

但把这种心态推得太远，也有危险。它可能让人期待，每个问题都有清楚的解释，每个重要问题都有明确的答案。

有时并没有。

有时我们的信息不够。有时多个原因同时起作用。有时一项政策在不同方面既有帮助也有伤害。有时我们还在努力理解一个系统，它就已经改变了。

有时正确的答案，仅仅是我们现在还不知道。

这个答案让人不舒服。

二元思维消除了这种不适。如果我认定资本主义好、社会主义坏，就不再需要逐一考察每一种制度。如果我认定美国对、中国错，或者反过来，就不再需要仔细看待每个国家的成功与失败。如果我认定 AI 不是救赎就是灾难，每一个新进展都会变成证据，支持我早已选定的故事。

这就是为什么，我越来越觉得二元思维与其说关乎逻辑，不如说关乎确定性。

它以很低的心理成本，给了我们一个简单的世界模型。

当信念变成身份认同

当一个观念成为身份认同的一部分，问题就会更严重。

相信 Bitcoin 有用，与成为一个“Bitcoin 人”，是两回事。相信市场通常有效，与把自己定义为资本主义者，是两回事。热爱自己的国家，与需要认定自己的国家是对的，也是两回事。

一旦一个观念成为“我是谁”的一部分，改变想法的代价就会变高。

证据不再只是证据。它开始让人觉得事关自己。

如果我有一个投资观点，而新的事实削弱了它的依据，我应该能够更新判断。但如果承认这个观点错了，也意味着承认“像我这样的人”错了，激励就变了。此时，我不只是在评估信息，也是在为自己辩护。

同样的事情发生在政治、宗教、国籍、技术和文化中。

聪明并不能自动保护我们免受这种影响。有时，它反而让问题更严重，因为聪明人非常善于为自己本来就想相信的事情寻找理由。

理性可以变成律师，而不是法官。

摆脱二元思维，不意味着每个人都对一半

这里还有另一个陷阱。

拒绝二元思维，并不意味着真相总是恰好位于中间。那不过是用另一条规则代替思考。

有时，一方确实更正确。有时证据是六四开，有时是九一开。有时，一种行为就是错的。

理解一件事为什么发生，不需要为它开脱。对某些事实不确定，不意味着所有事实都是相对的。愿意理解另一种观点，不意味着所有观点都应该获得同等分量。

重点不是成为中间派。

重点是在理解问题之前，避免先决定答案。

当我听到一场争论被框定为 A 对 B 时，我越来越会试着在选择之前先问几个问题。

我们实际上想达成什么？

是什么机制产生了这个结果？

有哪些权衡与取舍？

这真的是一道二选一的问题吗？

双方有没有可能是在描述同一现实的不同部分？

哪些东西会随时间改变？

什么会让我改变想法？

还有，我维护这个立场，是因为证据支持它，还是因为我已经在情感上依恋于“我是相信这种观点的那类人”这一身份？

这些问题不会消除不确定性，但它们让不确定性更容易应对，而不必假装它不存在。

在更少的确定性中生活

我觉得，这在今天或许更重要，因为世界本身可能正在变得更难预测。

AI 一边提升个人能力，一边集中基础设施。它让生产变得更容易，却让注意力更难获取。它可能创造巨额财富，同时改变工作与收入之间的关系。政府的技术能力越来越强，而许多人正在失去对制度的信任。新技术可以让个人更独立，同时也为集中化的系统提供新的控制工具。

如果这一切都清楚地指向一个方向，事情会方便得多。

但事实并非如此。

我们可以支持市场，同时承认市场会失灵。我们可以相信每个人都应该获得医疗服务，同时坚持成本很重要。我们可以热爱自己的国家，同时向另一个国家学习。我们可以相信 AI 会创造巨大的繁荣，同时承认不知道这种繁荣会如何分配。我们可以相信 Bitcoin，同时继续追问什么证据会削弱这一论点。

坚定的信念不是问题。

问题在于，我们需要这些信念变成确定无疑的东西。

我过去以为，成熟意味着理解更多，因此对世界更加确定。我越来越觉得，情况可能几乎相反。研究的复杂系统越多，我越能看见有多少事情可以同时为真。

目标不是变得无法作决定。我们仍然必须行动，仍然必须投票、投资、建设，选择在哪里生活，决定如何养育子女，以及想要什么样的社会。

但我们可以行动，而不必假装无所不知。

我们可以有坚定的信念，同时为更新判断留出空间。

我们可以承认不确定性，而不变得被动。

我们可以认识到自己的不足，而不把它们当成永远定义自己的标签。

我们可以理解另一边的人，而不立即加入他们。

我们可以改变想法，而不把它视为失败。

二元思维很快就能给我们确定性。它告诉我们谁对、谁错，以及我们属于哪里。

这让人觉得安全。

但在一个越来越复杂的世界里，即使没有这种确定性也能继续行动的能力，可能要宝贵得多。

世界不需要我们更快地选边站。

它需要我们在选择之前，先理解问题。

阅读英文原文

I have been thinking a lot about how easily we divide the world into opposite sides.

Capitalism or socialism. China or America. Men or women. Work or laziness. Universal healthcare or fiscal responsibility. Bitcoin or fiat. AI will save us, or AI will destroy us.

We hear one argument and almost automatically reach for its opposite. If someone criticizes capitalism, people assume they support socialism. If someone says China does something well, people assume they are defending the Chinese government. If someone questions the meaning of work, people assume they are defending laziness. If someone supports broader access to healthcare, they are accused of ignoring cost; if they talk about cost, they are accused of not caring about people.

At first I thought this was simply a problem of binary thinking. But I increasingly think the deeper issue is certainty.

Binary thinking is attractive because certainty is attractive. The world is complicated, and complicated systems are uncomfortable. They force us to admit that two contradictory things can both be true, that people we dislike can still be right about something, that policies can solve one problem while creating another, and that sometimes we have to make decisions without knowing the outcome.

A binary framework removes much of that discomfort. Once we decide which side we are on, a lot of the thinking has already been done for us.

The problem is that reality does not become simpler just because we describe it that way.

Healthcare

Since reaching financial independence, I have spent more time studying subjects that I care about simply because I think they matter. Healthcare is one of them. I have also become more interested in anthropology and in the broader question of how societies decide what they owe one another.

Healthcare is especially interesting because the moral question seems simple at first. If a society has the doctors, medicine, equipment, and knowledge required to save someone, allowing that person to die because they cannot afford treatment seems absurd. We are talking about a human life. If treatment exists and can be provided, there is something deeply tragic about money being the only thing standing in the way.

That is why the argument for universal healthcare is so powerful.

But another fact remains true: healthcare is extremely expensive.

If we do not change anything about the system and simply promise to pay every bill, we have not really solved the problem. We have changed who pays it. Hospital pricing, drug pricing, administrative overhead, incentives, insurance structures, utilization and end-of-life care all still matter. A society can care deeply about keeping people alive and still face real limits on how much of its resources can be devoted to healthcare.

I find it strange that this becomes such a polarized debate. Supporting universal care does not require pretending that cost does not matter. Caring about cost does not require being indifferent to a sick person who cannot afford treatment.

Both realities exist at the same time.

The better question is not simply whether we support universal healthcare. It is how to make sure people can receive essential care while also building a system that can continue functioning financially. That is harder than choosing a side, but at least it is the real problem.

Capitalism and Socialism

The capitalism-versus-socialism debate often feels similar.

Modern countries are not pure examples of either system. America has private markets, but it also has Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, public education, deposit insurance, agricultural subsidies, government research, infrastructure spending and a huge public sector. China has state ownership and industrial policy, but it also has private businesses, billionaires, capital markets, entrepreneurs and intense competition.

Most countries are mixtures because society itself is a mixture.

Markets solve some problems very well. Governments solve some problems markets do not solve well. Governments also fail. Markets fail. Families, communities, charities, churches and local institutions do things that neither governments nor markets can easily replace.

So I care less and less about whether a country calls itself capitalist, socialist or something else. The label is not the outcome.

What matters is what actually happens to people.

Can ordinary people build a decent life? Can they own things? Can they create businesses? Can they raise children? Can they speak? Can they move? Can they participate in society without constant fear? Can powerful institutions abuse them without consequences? When the system makes a serious mistake, can that mistake be corrected?

The worst failures of governments are usually concrete, not theoretical. Innocent people are persecuted. Wealth is taken unfairly. Wars are started. Power is abused. People are silenced. Institutions protect themselves instead of the people they are supposed to serve.

Every major country has examples of this. East and West both do. No civilization is exempt simply because it uses the right ideology to describe itself.

I grew up Chinese and have spent a large part of my adult life in America. To me, it should be completely normal for a Chinese person to say that America does something better, or for an American to say that China does something better. That should not threaten either person's identity.

In fact, being able to recognize the strengths of the side you are supposed to oppose is probably a useful test of whether you are thinking independently.

If a fact feels dangerous because of who is saying it, we may already have stopped evaluating the fact itself.

Work

The argument about work is another example.

Older generations often say younger people are lazy or no longer want to work. Younger people often look at the jobs available to them and think that many of those jobs are not worth the time, stress or sacrifice being asked of them.

The debate usually becomes moral very quickly.

One side says people have lost discipline. The other says the system is exploitative.

But before deciding who is right, I think we should ask a more basic question: what exactly is work for?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects total U.S. employment to grow 3.1 percent between 2024 and 2034, from about 170 million to 175.2 million jobs. Most of the projected gains are in healthcare and social assistance and in professional, scientific and technical services. Several sectors are expected to lose jobs, with much of that decline concentrated in retail trade.

That tells us something about the labor market, but not nearly enough about how people will actually live.

Even if the United States has 175 million jobs ten years from now, what kind of jobs are they? What do they pay? How many hours do they require? How stable are they? Where are they located? Can people support housing, healthcare, children and retirement with them? How much bargaining power do workers have? How many people actually want the jobs being created?

A job count is not the same thing as human welfare.

More importantly, a job is not the same thing as work, and work is not the same thing as value.

Raising children is work. Caring for elderly parents is work. Growing food is work. Maintaining a home is work. Helping people in your community is work. Studying something difficult is work. Creating software, writing, making art or teaching someone can create value even when no employer is involved.

Employment is one way of organizing labor and distributing income. It is not the only form of meaningful human activity.

Somewhere along the way, modern society tied several things together very tightly: work, income, identity, dignity and usefulness. Losing a job can therefore feel like losing more than money. People feel embarrassed. They feel less valuable. They may not know how to answer the question, "What do you do?"

That is a strange amount of meaning to place on an employment contract.

I am not arguing that people should do nothing. Almost everything we enjoy comes from someone's effort. Food has to be produced. Houses have to be built. Roads have to be maintained. Sick people need care. Children need to be raised. Communities need attention.

But work is a means through which we create and maintain a good life. It should not become the purpose of life itself.

This matters more as AI develops. It is very possible that future technology will allow society to produce more goods and services with fewer hours of human labor. If that happens, then preserving unnecessary labor just because we are used to organizing society around jobs would be a strange definition of progress.

The goal should not be to make sure everyone stays busy.

The goal should be to make it easier for people to live well, contribute, create, care for others and have meaningful lives.

AI and the Certainty of Value

This leads to something about AI that I find especially interesting.

AI is not only changing what people can create. It may also be changing the certainty with which value can be created deliberately.

Take Disney.

A company like Disney can spend hundreds of millions of dollars on a movie because it has built a system over decades. It can hire experienced writers, animators, actors, producers and marketers. It has distribution. It has brand recognition. It can test ideas, study audiences and put a finished product in front of millions of people.

None of that guarantees success. Disney can still make a bad movie or misjudge what people want. But the system reduces uncertainty. A large company can spend enough money, hire enough experienced people and use enough distribution power to increase the probability that something valuable will come out the other side.

Part of what capital buys is not only quality. It buys a degree of certainty.

Now imagine a random seventeen-year-old with AI tools.

He has no studio, no film school background, no marketing department and no real business plan. Maybe he makes something because he thinks it is funny. He posts it online, and somehow millions of people watch it.

He may have created more entertainment value in one evening than a professional team deliberately trying to do the same thing.

But there is another part of the story: he may not know why it worked. He may not be able to repeat it.

That is the part I find important.

AI is making it easier for people to create things that previously required money, teams and specialized skills. That expands the number of people who can produce something valuable. But when millions of people suddenly gain that ability, it may also become harder to know in advance which creation will matter.

Our ability to create value accidentally may be increasing faster than our ability to create value intentionally.

This is different from saying that value is disappearing. In many areas, the opposite may be happening. There may be far more useful software, entertainment, knowledge, art and experimentation than before.

What may be declining is the certainty that effort, capital and experience lead predictably to value.

A company can spend more money and still be ignored. An unknown creator can spend almost nothing and reach millions of people. An experienced professional may still have an enormous advantage, but that advantage no longer guarantees the same degree of separation from everyone else.

The scarce things may simply be moving.

When producing content becomes easier, attention becomes more important.

When information becomes abundant, trust matters more.

When technical execution becomes cheaper, judgment and taste become more valuable.

When everyone can create, knowing what is worth creating becomes harder.

This changes more than entertainment. For a long time, we have relied on relationships that felt relatively stable: more education tends to lead to better work, more experience tends to make someone more valuable, more employees allow a company to produce more, and more capital makes certain projects possible.

Those relationships will not disappear overnight. But they may weaken.

A small team can now do work that once required a much larger company. A young person can gain access to tools that used to belong only to professionals. A researcher outside a major institution can work with capabilities that previously required a laboratory or large organization.

This creates more opportunity, but it can also create more uncertainty.

People may become more capable while being less certain about how those capabilities will translate into income. Companies may become more productive while being less certain that their products will hold attention. Society may produce more value overall while making it harder to know who will capture that value.

That is not clearly good or bad. It is a change in the structure of the economy.

What Does It Mean to Win?

The same problem appears when people talk about AI competition between countries.

We often hear that America must beat China in AI, or that China must catch up with America. The language sounds similar to the nuclear arms race.

But what exactly does winning mean?

If America builds the most powerful AI models in the world but the result is a permanent arms race that makes the world less stable, is that winning?

If China develops extraordinary AI capabilities but does so through a system that requires greater concentration of power and surveillance, is that winning?

If AI makes GDP much larger but weakens the bargaining power of ordinary workers, should we automatically call that success?

If AI allows us to produce more while using much less human labor, but our social system still requires people to have jobs in order to obtain income, healthcare and dignity, then the technology may have succeeded while the institutions around it failed.

Before asking who wins, it is worth asking what game we are actually playing.

That question is useful far beyond AI.

A country can become richer while its people become less secure.

A company can become more efficient while its product becomes worse.

A healthcare system can spend more money while delivering poor outcomes.

A person can earn more while having less time, less freedom and less happiness.

The metric is not always the goal.

AI Can Centralize and Decentralize at the Same Time

AI itself is another place where the binary framework breaks down.

Some people describe AI as democratizing because a single person can now access capabilities that used to belong to large organizations. That is true. An individual can increasingly use AI as a programmer, researcher, translator, designer, analyst and tutor. It is difficult to overstate how much capability that can place in the hands of one person.

At the same time, building the most advanced AI systems requires enormous amounts of capital, chips, electricity, infrastructure and data-center capacity. Those resources are highly concentrated.

So AI can decentralize capability while centralizing infrastructure.

There is no contradiction.

The same technology can give individuals more power while also increasing the power of the organizations that control the largest models and computing resources.

Blockchain is different in architecture, but the broader lesson is similar. Technology does not automatically produce a political outcome. It changes what is possible. Institutions, incentives and ownership determine how that possibility is used.

Why We Like Certainty

I understand why people dislike uncertainty.

I have always been someone who responds to problems by trying to identify what I can change. If I do something badly, my instinct is to reflect, correct it and improve. I have never found my own shortcomings particularly frightening if I believe I can act on them.

That mindset is useful because it turns failure into something temporary.

But there is a danger in extending that mindset too far. It can create the expectation that every problem has a clear explanation and every important question has a clear answer.

Sometimes they do not.

Sometimes we do not have enough information. Sometimes several causes are operating at once. Sometimes a policy is both helpful and harmful in different ways. Sometimes the system changes while we are still trying to understand it.

Sometimes the correct answer is simply that we do not know yet.

That answer is uncomfortable.

Binary thinking removes that discomfort. If I decide that capitalism is good and socialism is bad, I no longer have to examine every institution individually. If I decide America is right and China is wrong, or the reverse, I no longer need to look carefully at each country's successes and failures. If I decide AI is either salvation or disaster, every new development becomes evidence for a story I have already chosen.

This is why I increasingly think binary thinking is less about logic and more about certainty.

It gives us a simple model of the world at a very low psychological cost.

When Beliefs Become Identity

The problem becomes worse when an idea becomes part of our identity.

There is a difference between believing that Bitcoin is useful and being a "Bitcoin person." There is a difference between believing markets are generally effective and defining yourself as a capitalist. There is a difference between loving your country and needing your country to be right.

Once an idea becomes part of who we are, changing our mind becomes expensive.

Evidence is no longer just evidence. It feels personal.

If I hold an investment thesis and new facts weaken it, I should be able to update. But if admitting the thesis was wrong also means admitting that "people like me" were wrong, the incentive changes. Now I am not just evaluating information. I am defending myself.

The same thing happens in politics, religion, nationality, technology and culture.

Intelligence does not automatically protect us from this. In some cases it makes the problem worse because intelligent people are very good at finding reasons for things they already want to believe.

Reason can become a lawyer instead of a judge.

Escaping Binary Thinking Does Not Mean Everyone Is Half Right

There is another trap here.

Rejecting binary thinking does not mean the truth always sits exactly in the middle. That would just be another rule replacing thought.

Sometimes one side really is more correct. Sometimes the evidence is 60/40. Sometimes it is 90/10. Sometimes an action is simply wrong.

Understanding why something happened does not require excusing it. Being uncertain about some facts does not mean all facts are relative. Being willing to understand another perspective does not mean all perspectives deserve equal weight.

The point is not to become a centrist.

The point is to avoid deciding the answer before understanding the problem.

When I hear a debate framed as A versus B, I increasingly try to ask a few questions before choosing.

What are we actually trying to achieve?

What mechanism is producing the result?

What are the trade-offs?

Is this really a binary choice?

Can both sides be describing different parts of the same reality?

What changes over time?

What would make me change my mind?

And am I defending this position because the evidence supports it, or because I have become emotionally attached to being the kind of person who believes it?

These questions do not eliminate uncertainty. They make uncertainty more manageable without pretending it does not exist.

Living With Less Certainty

I suspect this matters more now because the world itself may be becoming harder to predict.

AI is increasing individual capability while concentrating infrastructure. It is making production easier while making attention harder to capture. It may create enormous wealth while changing the relationship between work and income. Governments are becoming more technologically capable at the same time that many people are losing trust in institutions. New technologies can make individuals more independent while also giving centralized systems new tools of control.

It would be convenient if all of this pointed clearly in one direction.

It does not.

We can support markets and still recognize market failures. We can believe healthcare should be available to everyone while insisting that costs matter. We can love our country while learning from another. We can believe AI will create enormous prosperity while admitting that we do not know how that prosperity will be distributed. We can believe in Bitcoin while still asking what evidence would make the thesis weaker.

Strong beliefs are not the problem.

The problem is needing those beliefs to become certainty.

I used to think maturity meant understanding more and therefore becoming more certain about the world. I increasingly think it may be almost the opposite. The more complicated systems I study, the more I see how many things can be true at the same time.

The goal is not to become unable to make decisions. We still have to act. We still have to vote, invest, build, choose where to live, decide how to raise our families and decide what kind of society we want.

But we can act without pretending that we know everything.

We can have conviction while leaving room to update.

We can admit uncertainty without becoming passive.

We can recognize our own shortcomings without turning them into permanent identities.

We can understand the people on the other side without immediately joining them.

We can change our minds without treating it as defeat.

Binary thinking gives us certainty quickly. It tells us who is right, who is wrong and where we belong.

That feels safe.

But in a world that is becoming more complicated, the ability to function without that certainty may be far more valuable.

The world does not need us to choose sides faster.

It needs us to understand the question before we choose.
